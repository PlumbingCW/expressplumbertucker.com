Do You Need a Plumbing Permit in Tucker, GA? A Practical Guide

The honest answer to "do I need a permit" is that it depends on what you are doing and where. This guide explains who decides, what to ask and what to expect, so you do not have to guess or skip a step that matters at resale.

We do not issue permits, and we do not guess your result. We describe the work, and you confirm with the authority.

Who decides in Tucker

The City of Tucker Building and Permitting office handles permits and stage inspections for work inside the city. DeKalb County handles the Fire Marshal, streets, and water and sewer. If your project touches the public water or sewer connection, the county is also involved.

The City of Tucker permit team is the right first call. Look up the current permit desk number on the city's website before you call.

Why licensing matters

Georgia licenses plumbers as journeymen and master plumbers, with Class I and Class II master licenses and a statewide master license. Only licensed master plumbers can operate a plumbing business, and the licensing board does not register companies. The regulator is the Secretary of State's Division of Master and Journeyman Plumbers. When you hire someone, ask about the license and verify it. Our page on plumbing installation explains how we describe scope for permit questions.

What an inspector looks at

Inspections generally check that work matches the plumbing code in force, that connections are tight and tested, that venting is sound and that safety devices are in place. Georgia's state minimum plumbing code is built on the International Plumbing Code with Georgia amendments. We have not verified which edition applies today, so ask the permit team.

If you skip a permit

Unpermitted work can create trouble at resale, with insurance claims and when an inspector finds it later. Even for work that does not need one, keep records of what was done, who did it and when. A licensed plumber who describes the work in writing makes that easy.

Before you dig

Georgia 811 is free and asks for at least three business days of notice before digging. It applies to sewer and water line work as well as landscaping. See water service line repair for how that fits a service line project.

A short script for your permit call

Calling a permit desk is easier with a plan. Give the address, describe the work in one sentence and ask three questions: does this need a permit, who pulls it, and what inspections follow. Write down the name of the person and the date. If the answer is unclear, ask them to point you to the page where the rule is written.

Who pulls the permit

In many cases the licensed contractor pulls the permit for plumbing work, and the homeowner is not the applicant. Homeowner permits may be allowed for work on your own primary residence, with limits. The rules are set locally, so the permit team is the source for what applies to your address.

Either way, ask the plumber how the permit is handled before you sign. A contractor who says "we do not do permits" for work that needs them is a red flag worth pausing on.

Keep your own file

Keep copies of the permit, the invoice, the inspection result and photos of the work before walls are closed. They are useful at resale, for insurance questions and when the next plumber asks what is behind the wall. A folder on your phone is enough.

Because rules are local and change, treat this page as a guide to the questions, not as a permit decision. The permit team makes the call, and a short phone conversation settles it.

Quick reference for homeowners

If you are replacing like for like, such as a faucet or a toilet, you will probably have a simple question for the permit team. If you are moving fixtures, adding a bathroom, changing gas piping or repiping, expect more review. If you are working with a contractor, ask who is responsible for the permit and get the answer in writing.

Permit rules protect safety and records. They also protect you if a problem shows up later, since a permitted and inspected job is easier to document.

Sources and standards

The International Plumbing Code (IPC) with Georgia amendments is the base of the state minimum code. Licensing information comes from the Secretary of State's Division of Master and Journeyman Plumbers, and permit rules come from the City of Tucker.

Local note: the line between city and county

Inside Tucker, the city permits the work and the county owns the water and sewer system. In Gwinnett towns like Norcross and Lilburn, different offices apply, so a permit answer for one town does not transfer.
