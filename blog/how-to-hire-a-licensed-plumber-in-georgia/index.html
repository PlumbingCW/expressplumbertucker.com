What a Georgia plumbing license means

Georgia licenses plumbers at several levels: journeyman, Class I master, Class II master and statewide master. Only licensed master plumbers can have a plumbing business, and the licensing board does not register companies. The regulator is the Secretary of State's Division of Master and Journeyman Plumbers.

Ask to see the license number, and check it with the state. A license that matches the name on the truck and the invoice is the first thing a real company shows.

Emergencies and pressure

In a flood or backup, a plumber who explains the options calmly is better than one who rushes you to sign. A reliable company will stop the damage first, such as closing a valve, and then explain the repair. Our approach to emergencies is described on burst pipe repair, and we say plainly when a job is outside what we do.

Be wary of claims you cannot check

Reviews, awards and years in business are claims. Check them where you can, and treat missing information as a reason to ask, not to assume. We do not publish ratings we cannot back up.

Hiring for larger jobs

For a repipe, sewer replacement or a remodel, get more than one assessment. A diagnosis that includes a camera or pressure test is more trustworthy than a quote from a glance. For sewer decisions, a sewer camera inspection gives you evidence to compare quotes against.

Questions to ask on the phone

Before anyone comes to the house

  • ✓What is the license number, and who holds it?
  • ✓Will you give me the price before the work starts?
  • ✓How do you diagnose the problem before recommending a repair?
  • ✓Who will actually do the work, and are they licensed?
  • ✓What does your warranty cover and for how long?

What a good visit looks like

The plumber introduces themselves, looks at the problem and asks questions. They explain what they found, show it to you if possible and give options. They state a price and wait for your approval. They protect your floors and clean up.

If you are rushed, pushed to sign on the spot or told that only the most expensive option will work, pause. Ask for the findings in writing and, for a large job, get a second assessment.

Mistakes homeowners make

  • Hiring the first number that appears in a search without checking anything.
  • Paying large amounts in cash with no written scope.
  • Letting work start before a price is agreed.
  • Skipping the permit question on larger jobs.
  • Not keeping the invoice and warranty paperwork.

None of this is unusual. A reliable plumber expects the questions and answers them without fuss. It also protects you after the job, when you need records for insurance or resale.

Emergency versus planned work

In an emergency, speed and stopping the damage matter most, and the plumber may need to act before a full written scope exists. Ask for a verbal price and a short description before work starts. For planned work, take your time. Get written scopes, compare what each includes and ask about the schedule and warranty.

Whichever kind of job it is, the best sign of a trustworthy plumber is that they explain things clearly and do not mind questions. Plumbing is a trade with a lot of jargon, and a good one translates.

Quick reference when you have five minutes

Ask for the license number. Ask for the price before work starts. Ask how the plumber will diagnose the problem. Ask who does the work. Ask about the warranty. If two plumbers disagree about the cause, ask each to show you the evidence. A camera image, a pressure reading or a meter test settles more arguments than opinion.

Write down the name of the person you spoke to and the time. If a dispute arises later, a note helps.

Sources and standards

License categories and the rule that only licensed master plumbers can operate a plumbing business come from the Secretary of State's Division of Master and Journeyman Plumbers. Code references are to the International Plumbing Code (IPC).
